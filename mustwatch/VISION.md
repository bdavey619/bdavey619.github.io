# Must Watch — Product Vision

*Last updated: April 16, 2026*

---

## 1. The Problem

There are roughly 15–20 major sports events on any given weekday during the MLB and NBA regular seasons. Playoffs push that number even higher.

Most sports products respond to this by giving you more: more scores, more headlines, more notifications, more team-specific alerts. The underlying assumption is that a sports fan's core problem is access to information. It isn't.

The actual problem is **signal in the context of real life**. Specifically:

- **Time is the scarce resource, not information.** A busy person does not have four evenings a week to spend on sports. They might have one — or half of one. The question isn't what happened. It's whether anything is worth the tradeoff.
- **Too many games, no priority signal.** A score aggregator tells you what happened. It doesn't tell you what mattered.
- **Favorite-team defaults are not the same as must-watch.** Your team's Tuesday regular season game is probably not the most compelling event of the week. Most products can't tell you that — or won't.
- **Staying informed requires active media consumption.** If you aren't watching SportsCenter, checking Twitter, or following beat reporters, you risk missing the stories that everyone will be talking about tomorrow. There's no clean shortcut.
- **No one is helping you allocate attention.** ESPN helps you browse. Beat writers help you go deep. Social media surfaces noise and heat. None of these answer: *what is actually worth my time this week?*

The result is that a casual-but-engaged sports fan either over-invests (watches too much, follows too many accounts) or under-invests (misses the games that turn out to matter, feels out of the loop). Both failures stem from the same gap: no trusted source is telling them where to spend their limited time.

---

## 2. The Insight

Not all games are equal. This seems obvious, but very few sports products act like it's true.

A game between two teams in active playoff races, featuring a historic pitching duel or a revenge series, is categorically more compelling than a mid-table team grinding out an inconsequential win — regardless of which teams the reader happens to follow.

The more important insight: **the thing that makes a game must-watch is storyline, not quality.** A close game means nothing if nothing is riding on it. A blowout with historic implications is appointment viewing. Scores are outputs. Narratives are the reason people watch.

The editorial instinct that experienced sports fans develop over years — *this week, this game is the one you need to watch* — can be formalized. Stakes, competitive balance, recent momentum, star presence, narrative flags: these are detectable from data, not just vibes. Ranking across those dimensions produces a defensible, non-obvious list. Not a list of popular games. A list of *important* games.

The further insight: a **weekly** format is the right shape for this. Nightly recaps already exist. Real-time scores already exist. What doesn't exist is a reliable Monday-morning read that answers: *here's where the interesting sports stories are this week, ranked*. That's a planning tool, not a feed.

---

## 2a. The Real Job To Be Done

Must Watch exists to help someone make a decision: *is this worth my time, or not?*

That's not the same as "what happened in sports." It's a much more personal calculation.

The user is a busy adult — a professional, a parent, someone with competing obligations. Their evening could go to the game, or to dinner with a partner, or to finally sleeping. Watching sports is not a default. It is a choice they have to make and sometimes defend.

What they need is **confidence**. Not a data dump. Not a highlights reel. Confidence that a specific game is worth planning around — worth turning down another commitment, worth staying up for, worth being the person who watched it when everyone else is talking about it Monday.

That confidence comes from storylines, not scores. From knowing that this particular game is part of something — a streak, a rivalry, a race, a moment that will be referenced for years. ESPN shows you your team. Social media shows you what's on fire right now. Neither tells you: *this is the game worth fighting for on your calendar.*

Must Watch does.

The job is not to inform. It's to give someone the confidence to say: *I'm watching Thursday night. I've done my research. This one matters.*

---

## 3. The Product

Must Watch is a weekly ranking engine that looks at every upcoming MLB and NBA game for the next seven days and surfaces the five most narratively compelling matchups — with specific, evidence-based reasoning for why each made the list.

It is, in plain terms: **a trusted sports editor in product form**.

Not a sports news site. Not a score tracker. Not a team-specific brief. A weekly publication that answers one question clearly:

> *Which sports events this week are actually worth your attention?*

The scoring engine is the product. The rankings are derived from a defined framework — stakes, competitive balance, momentum, star power, narrative context — applied consistently, without favoritism toward big markets or popular teams.

The explanations are also the product. Every ranked event comes with 2–3 sentences that say something specific and verifiable about why it earned its position. No generic sports language. No empty hype. If the explanation could apply to any game, it gets rewritten until it can't.

---

## 4. Who It Is For

The target user is someone who is genuinely interested in sports but does not live inside sports media.

More specifically:

- **Active after work.** Has a real life that competes with sports for evening and weekend time. Cannot watch three hours of games per night. Watching sports is a choice they make deliberately, not a default.
- **Balancing real tradeoffs.** A Tuesday evening game competes with family time, a partner, sleep, or something else that matters. They are not looking for more sports — they are looking for the right sports.
- **Not consuming daily sports news.** Doesn't have ESPN notifications on. Doesn't follow 40 beat reporters. Probably checks scores occasionally but is not tracking narratives week-to-week.
- **Still wants to stay meaningfully informed.** Cares about the sport beyond just their team. Wants to know when something important is happening, even if they didn't see it coming. Dreads finding out they missed the game everyone is talking about.
- **Interested in multiple sports.** Watches MLB and NBA (and probably other sports), but not necessarily all equally. Open to being pointed toward the most compelling game regardless of which sport it's in.
- **Values their time over entertainment volume.** Would rather watch one game that really matters than half-watch three games out of obligation.

The user's core fear is not missing sports — it is **missing the moment**. The game that becomes a story. The performance that gets referenced for years. The context that makes next week's game make sense. Must Watch is built to prevent that.

This is not a product for the sports obsessive who monitors every transaction and watches four games simultaneously. It's for the person who wants to stay connected to sports without it being a second job.

## 5. Why It Is Better Than the Status Quo

The dominant tools for a casual-but-engaged sports fan are:

**ESPN app / score aggregators:** Good for checking results. Not designed for curation or decision-making. The front page is driven by recency and popularity, not importance. You get a live ticker, not a ranked week.

**Favorite-team feeds:** Only useful if you follow one team deeply. Blind to the rest of the sports landscape. A Padres fan using a Padres app will never learn that this week's most compelling game is in Boston.

**SportsCenter / sports news:** Requires active consumption and time investment. Surfaces stories reactively (after games, after controversies) rather than prospectively (before a game worth watching). Heavy on takes, light on ranked signal.

**Social media / Twitter:** Fast and reactive, but noisy. Surfaces heat more than substance. Requires following the right accounts and filtering a high-noise feed.

The fundamental difference: **those products optimize for browsing and engagement. Must Watch optimizes for decision-making.**

They want you to stay in the app. Must Watch wants to give you a clear answer and let you close the tab.

## 6. How Someone Uses It

The expected pattern is simple and low-friction:

**Monday morning, 5 minutes.** Read the weekly list. Note which games are high-stakes. Put one or two on the calendar — actually block the time.

**Before a high-stakes evening, 2 minutes.** Check the list again. Decide whether tonight's game is worth making time for. Have a reason ready if you need one.

**Passive awareness the rest of the week.** You already know what's important. You can follow along without needing to monitor daily coverage.

The goal is to make it so that a user who checks Must Watch once a week and doesn't follow sports otherwise still knows:
- What games matter
- Why they matter
- Which one to watch if they can only watch one

That's the test. If someone reads the top 5 and says "I had no idea this was happening — I'll make time for that," the product has done its job.

The deeper test: does the list give someone enough confidence to *plan around it*? To tell their partner, "I want to watch Thursday — it's a good one." If the explanation isn't specific enough to make that case, it isn't specific enough.

## 7. Product Principles

**Respect the user's time.** The product should be readable in under three minutes. No padding. No hot takes. No noise.

**Curation over clutter.** Five ranked events with specific reasons is better than fifteen events with scores. The act of not including something is part of the editorial value.

**Opinionated ranking over passive feeds.** The product takes a position. This game ranks above that one, across sports, for specific reasons. Hedging and equal-weight treatment are failures of editorial nerve.

**Explainability builds trust.** Users should be able to see why an event ranked where it did. If the reasoning isn't specific and defensible, the ranking isn't earned.

**Story setup, not game prediction.** We score the narrative going into a game — stakes, momentum, context, history. A compelling setup that produces a blowout is not a product failure. We're a preview engine, not a guarantee.

**Objectivity over fandom.** Rankings are sport-agnostic and team-agnostic. A game should not rank higher because the reader follows one of the teams. Big markets and big-name brands do not get a handicap.

## 8. Long-Term Direction

The current product is weekly and covers MLB and NBA. That scope is intentional — sharp coverage of two leagues is more useful than shallow coverage of ten.

A few natural extensions, in rough order of priority:

**"Must Watch Tonight"** — a lightweight daily companion to the weekly edition. Answers the same question for a single evening instead of a week. Lower overhead than the weekly version; probably a structured summary format rather than a full ranking.

**Cross-sport expansion** — NHL is the natural next sport. Playoff structure, high stakes, clear rivalries, good data availability. Premier League has strong narrative infrastructure and an underserved US audience. PGA and ATP require different scoring models (individual, not team) and are lower priority.

**Intraweek updates** — a major injury, a trade, a streak reaching historic territory: some stories break mid-week and change what matters. A lightweight update format could surface those without replacing the weekly edition.

**Mobile-first experience** — the current product produces web output. A mobile surface (notification, widget, or clean mobile page) would match the actual use pattern better — checking from a phone on Monday morning before work.

The long-term ceiling: a personalized sports newspaper front page where Must Watch is the flagship module and the product expands to include standings context, player storylines, and league-specific briefs. If personalization is ever added, it should be a filter (opt out of sports you don't follow), not a bias (boost my team). The ranking stays objective.
