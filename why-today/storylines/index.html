  • updated Venus never got to keep a moon long enough for anyone to see it Sep 13–Sep 18 · 3×

    recent research finding, now peer-reviewed University of California, Riverside

    New modeling from UC Riverside's Stephen Kane finds that almost any moon Venus once had would have spiraled inward and been torn apart by the planet's own gravity within about a billion years - explaining why, unlike Earth and Mars, Venus has none today.

    What does it mean for a planet to be built in a way that guarantees it destroys its own moon?

    Sep 18 The finding cleared peer review, publishing in The Astrophysical Journal. Kane validated his model by first reproducing Earth and its own moon's real orbital history, then ran the same physics on Venus - every hypothetical moon crashed rather than escaped.

    2 earlier updates
    • Sep 16UC Riverside puts a number on it: a moon half our Moon's mass would survive ~1.7 billion years before Venus's rotation pulled it apart; twice that mass, only ~30 million years. NASA's DAVINCI mission could look for atmospheric traces.
    • Sep 13UC Riverside modeling finds any moon Venus once had would spiral inward and be torn apart by its gravity within about a billion years - a survivable moon needs a fast-spinning early Venus and a moon no heavier than ours.
