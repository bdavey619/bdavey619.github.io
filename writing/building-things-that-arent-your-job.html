Building things that aren't your job

My day job is finance. I spend most of my time on forecasts, metrics, and figuring out how a growing company spends its money. It's good work. I like it.

But I also like building things — tools, dashboards, anything that takes messy information and makes it useful. Not because I'm trying to become an engineer. Because I keep running into problems where the solution is a small piece of software that doesn't exist yet.

The fish market near my house sends a weekly email with prices. I wanted to know if $18/lb for yellowtail was a good deal, so I built a dashboard that could tell me. That kind of thing.

What I've noticed is that the hardest part is never the technical execution. It's figuring out what the thing should do. What data matters? What's noise? What would actually change how I make a decision? Those are the same questions I ask at work every day. Finance people — really anyone who thinks in systems — tend to be decent at this, because we already spend our time modeling how things connect.

The tools for building are better than they've ever been. That part is exciting. But the tools don't tell you what's worth building. That still comes from paying attention to your own problems and being honest about what would actually help.

Start with something you know well. Build the thing you wish existed. Ship it rough and improve it later. That's the whole method.
